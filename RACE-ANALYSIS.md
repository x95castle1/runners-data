# Chicago Marathon — what happened

**Sunday 11 October 2026. Finished in 6:47:57.**

Written the day after, from the Apple Health export. The pre-race plan is in
[MARATHONPLAN.md](MARATHONPLAN.md); this is the post-mortem.

**The finding, up front:** resting heart rate on race morning was **69 bpm against
a 53 baseline** — the highest reading in the file — and HRV was **29 ms against a
46 baseline**. The race was run by a body that was already sixteen beats down
before the gun. Everything else follows from that.

## It was not the weather

The first hypothesis, and the data rules it out cleanly. Dew point is what
matters in humidity, not air temperature:

| Run | Dew point | Pace |
|---|---|---|
| Sep 26 — 14 mi | 49°F | 12:34 |
| **Race — 27 mi** | **56°F** | **15:06** |
| Jul 30 — 14 mi | 56°F | 13:00 |
| Aug 29 — 20 mi | 60°F | 13:39 |
| Aug 22 — 18 mi | 63°F | 13:04 |
| Jul 25 — 13.1 mi | 63°F | 12:44 |
| Sep 12 — 20 mi | 64°F | 13:51 |
| Sep 19 — 22 mi | 69°F | 13:59 |
| Aug 8 — 16 mi | 70°F | 13:20 |
| Sep 5 — 15 mi | 72°F | 14:47 |

Race day was the **second-best** set of conditions of the whole block, and
thirteen degrees of dew point kinder than the 22-miler. The pre-race forecast of
67°F rising to 78°F was wrong; the morning was better than predicted.

Also ruled out: **pacing** (slower than planned, not faster), **the course**
(flat), and **a late collapse** (the cost was elevated from mile one).

---

## It was there before the start

The clearest comparison in the dataset:

| | Race, Oct 11 | Aug 29, 20-miler |
|---|---|---|
| First 9 miles | 14:44/mi at **HR 136** | 14:20/mi at **HR 120** |
| Dew point | 56°F | 60°F |

**Same pace. Sixteen beats per minute higher. Better conditions. Fully tapered.**

That is not a fuelling error or a pacing error — those appear at mile 18, not
mile 1.

---

## The evidence

### Resting heart rate and HRV

Baseline, mid-August to end of September: **resting HR 53 bpm, HRV 46 ms.**

| Date | Resting HR | HRV |
|---|---|---|
| Oct 8 | 54 | 56 |
| Oct 9 | 54 | 56 |
| Oct 10 *(expo)* | 57 (+4) | 60 |
| **Oct 11 — race** | **69 (+16)** | **29 (−17)** |
| Oct 12 | 59 (+6) | 52 |

Resting heart rate was already drifting up on the expo day while HRV still looked
normal. Overnight, both fell apart. A sharp rise in resting HR with a sharp drop
in HRV is the recognised signature of a body fighting something, badly
under-recovered, or both.

**+16 bpm at rest. +16 bpm at mile 5. It is the same sixteen beats**, carried
through all 27 miles. The heart rate wasn't behaving strangely during the race —
it started there.

### The expo

Daily steps, race week:

| Oct 7 | Oct 8 | Oct 9 | **Oct 10 (expo)** | Oct 11 (race) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5,272 | 5,811 | 9,229 | **12,526** | 69,878 |

Roughly five miles on the feet the day before, more than double the Oct 7–8
baseline, on the one day whose job was to do nothing.

### What it felt like

> "I didn't sleep well… I didn't feel the best the night before. The first miles
> felt tough, but I never really felt good the entire race. Usually around mile
> 6–7 I get into a nice groove."

Four descriptions of one thing: poor sleep, five miles of expo walking, feeling
off the night before, and an elevated heart rate at easy pace from mile one.

---

## Why the heart rate behaves this way

When the immune system is working, or sleep is short and fluid is low, the heart
compensates by beating more often for the same output — plasma volume drops,
stroke volume drops, rate rises to cover it. Every pace costs more than it
should. There is no training through it and no pushing past it on the day.

Those sixteen beats put the effort at roughly a 13:30 pace while actually running
14:44. That accounts for most of the missing hour.

---

## What it does and doesn't mean

**It did not measure fitness.**

- Nine days before the race: **14 miles at 12:34/mi**, the best long-run pace of
  the block
- VO2 max on race morning: **41.3**, right at the peak of 42.1
- Jan–Mar → Jul–Sep: **220 → 402 miles a quarter**, average HR 136 → 130,
  VO2 max 35.9 → 41.0
- Weight: **267 → 246 lb**

None of that is undone. It was all still there on Monday morning.

**The pre-race prediction was too optimistic anyway.** The 5:45–5:50 estimate
leaned too heavily on the April half marathon. Running Riegel off the Aug 29
twenty-miler instead — marathon-specific work rather than a shorter, faster
effort — gives **6:03**. So the gap between Sunday and a rested version of the
same runner is more like **30–45 minutes**, not the full hour against the plan.

---

## What to do differently

1. **The expo is a trap.** Go on Friday, send someone else, or go and sit down a
   lot. Legs up, feet off the floor. Highest-return thing available in the last
   48 hours and it costs nothing.
2. **Check resting HR and HRV the morning before.** Baseline is 53 bpm and 46 ms.
   The Oct 10 drift — resting HR up while HRV still looked fine — was an early
   warning that nobody was watching for. It is a number on the Watch.
3. **Nothing else changes.** The training block worked. The intensity
   distribution could be more polarised (60% easy / 40% moderate-hard, against a
   more productive 80/20) but that is a refinement, not a fix.

---

## For the record

26.99 miles, no stops, on a morning the body said no. That part isn't in any of
the tables above, and it's the part most people can't do.

The fitness doesn't expire. A spring half off this base, on a day that starts at
53 bpm instead of 69, is a completely different race.
